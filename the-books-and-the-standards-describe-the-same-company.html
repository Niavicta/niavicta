Follow the journey · Founder Diaries

The books and the standards are describing the same company.

Founders read the management books and resent the quality standards. Both ask for defined responsibility, real processes and written-down knowledge. They just speak different languages.

Founder Diaries Chani Galgut 9 November 2026

My first job was as a QA officer at a dental implant company in South Africa, and I took it without knowing what QA meant. Because of the high unemployment rates, I didn't ask any questions, and I pounced on the opportunity immediately.

Then I found out what the job actually was, and it was the best thing that could have happened to a curious person. When you are the QA officer, you are not out of place anywhere. You can walk into any department, and ask anybody what they are doing, and no one bats an eye. So I did, constantly, and that is where I first ran into the idea of systems thinking, and cross-departmental integration. I didn't have these fancy words to describe it at first, so I remember thinking that the QA department was like the spinal cord and nervous system of the company, and the bones, sort of like the skeleton, were responsible for making sure signals and information flow sensibly from one place to another.

When you are a QA officer, and you've worked at many companies and audited many more, you get to see that all companies are kind of the same on the inside. At least they should be if they're regulated companies, because that's kind of the whole point. Different products, different people, all supported by the same systems. In regulated industries, that sameness is already documented in standards like ISO 9001 and ISO 13485.

Meanwhile the business people were reading High Output Management, and Good to Great, and How the Mighty Fall, and The Hard Thing About Hard Things. I read them too, and every one of those books talks about processes. Defining responsibility and authority. Institutionalising your information so the company knows things independently of who happens to be in the building.

Which is what a quality management standard mandates you to do anyway. They just speak a different language.

This is when I realized that there are two types of thinking being smushed into one company, and the two groups of people who most needed to hear each other could not, because one of them says "institutional knowledge" and the other says "clause 4.2.4".

So I did a master's degree in international management, part time, while working, because I worked out that for my entire career I was going to be talking to business people who needed to listen to me if their business was going to work, and I could not keep expecting them to learn my vocabulary, so I went and learned theirs.

Then I moved to the Netherlands, to a company in Utrecht building a device that does autonomous robotic phlebotomy. The machine takes a 3D ultrasound image of the veins and arteries in your arm, reconstructs it, comes in with a robotic arm, puts the needle in at exactly the right angle, draws the blood, inverts the tubes, and puts a bandage on. The QA/RA department got it CE marked and through FDA clearance before the engineers had finished building it, which is a slightly show-off thing to say, and it is true, and it is only possible because the processes and the documentation were implemented, and the team was really diligent about following them.

That is the whole argument, actually. The early certification was possible because the structure was there first.

I think about that whenever I hear regulation described as a hurdle. In an unregulated industry, somebody phones a consultant once things have broken a little bit, and says, right, we would like to make this better now. In medical devices you are mandated to do it at the beginning. It's the same work, although one company gets told to do it before it hurts.

So when a founder tells me they have not got time for a quality system yet, what I hear is that they have not got time to define who is responsible for what, write down how the work is done, and keep a record of decisions. They would never say that sentence out loud, because they have read the books. They know that company would fall over.

You are not being asked to do extra work alongside building your company. You have been given the part of building a company that somebody already defined.
